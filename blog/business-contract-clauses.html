Important Clauses Every Business Contract Should Have

Legal Guides MBOSS Lexon LLP November 12, 2024 6 min read
📄
MBOSS LEXON LLP · LEGAL GUIDES

Why Contract Clauses Matter

A business contract is only as strong as its clauses. Many businesses enter into commercial agreements without understanding what they have agreed to — or worse, without key protective provisions. This creates exposure to disputes, financial losses and reputational damage.

Whether you are drafting a vendor agreement, a service contract, an MoU, or an NDA, certain clauses are non-negotiable from a risk management perspective. This guide covers the essential clauses that every business contract in India should include.

1. Scope of Work / Services

Every contract must clearly define what exactly is being done, delivered or provided. Vague scope provisions are the most common cause of commercial disputes.

Best practice: Define scope in a Schedule or Annexure with specific deliverables, specifications, timelines and acceptance criteria. Avoid general language like "any related services required."

2. Payment Terms

Payment clauses must cover: amount or fee structure, payment schedule, invoicing requirements, payment method, late payment interest, and consequences of non-payment.

Key provision: Include a specific late payment interest clause (e.g., 18% per annum on outstanding amounts) — this provides a remedy and incentivises timely payment.

3. Term and Termination

Every contract needs clear provisions on:

  • Contract duration — Start date, end date, and renewal mechanism
  • Termination for cause — Specific events entitling a party to terminate (material breach, insolvency, etc.)
  • Termination for convenience — Right to exit with notice even without breach (include notice period)
  • Consequences of termination — Outstanding payment obligations, asset return, survival of confidentiality

4. Confidentiality / NDA

Even in non-NDA contracts, a confidentiality clause is essential when the parties will share proprietary information, business data, pricing, customer lists or technical know-how.

Key elements: Definition of confidential information, exclusions (publicly available information, independently developed), duration of confidentiality obligations (typically 2-5 years post-termination), and permitted disclosures.

5. Intellectual Property Ownership

Who owns any IP created during the contract? This is critical in technology, content, design and consulting agreements.

Best practice: Explicitly state whether IP created belongs to the client (work-for-hire) or the service provider. If the provider retains background IP, specify the licence granted to the client.

6. Limitation of Liability

One of the most commercially important clauses — limits the maximum financial exposure of one or both parties under the contract.

Standard position: Liability limited to the fees paid under the contract in the 12 months preceding the claim. Exclude consequential, indirect, special and punitive damages.

Note: Some limitation of liability clauses are not enforceable in certain consumer-facing situations — legal advice is important when drafting these.

7. Indemnification

Indemnification clauses require one party to compensate the other for specified losses. Typical indemnities include:

  • IP infringement indemnity (service provider indemnifies client if their work infringes third-party IP)
  • Data breach indemnity
  • Gross negligence or wilful misconduct indemnity
  • Third-party claim indemnity

8. Representations and Warranties

Each party should represent and warrant key facts — that they have authority to enter the contract, that there are no conflicting obligations, that delivered work meets specifications, and that they will comply with applicable laws.

9. Dispute Resolution

How will disputes be resolved? Include:

  • Escalation procedure — Senior management negotiation before formal proceedings
  • Arbitration or Mediation clause — Preferred for commercial contracts for faster, private resolution
  • Seat of arbitration — Which city/institution
  • Governing law — Indian law typically
  • Jurisdiction — Which courts have jurisdiction if litigation becomes necessary

10. Force Majeure

A force majeure clause excuses performance when events beyond a party's reasonable control prevent it — natural disasters, pandemics, government action, strikes, etc.

Best practice: Define force majeure events clearly, specify notice requirements, and include a termination right if force majeure continues beyond a set period (e.g., 60 days).

11. Entire Agreement and Amendments

An entire agreement clause states that the written contract represents the complete agreement between the parties, superseding all prior negotiations, representations and understandings. This prevents parties from relying on pre-contract statements as contractual commitments.

Amendment provision: Require all amendments to be in writing and signed by authorised representatives of both parties.

12. Notices

Specify how formal notices must be given — physical address, email, and whether read/delivery receipts are required. This clause is critical for triggering contractual timelines (notice of termination, breach cure periods, etc.).

FAQ — Business Contracts
Is a verbal contract legally valid in India?+

Yes, oral contracts are generally legally valid in India under the Indian Contract Act 1872. However, they are extremely difficult to prove and enforce in disputes. Written contracts with clear terms are essential for any significant business relationship. Certain contracts (sale of immovable property, partnership deeds, etc.) legally require written form.

What is a material breach of contract?+

A material breach is a failure to perform a fundamental obligation under the contract — one that goes to the heart of the agreement and defeats the purpose of the contract. A material breach typically entitles the non-breaching party to treat the contract as terminated and claim damages. Minor or technical breaches do not rise to the level of material breach.
